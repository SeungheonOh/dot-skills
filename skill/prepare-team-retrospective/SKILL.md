# Turn a retrospective into a practical improvement packet

Deliver materials that help a team examine a specific period of work, distinguish observations from explanations, and choose manageable changes with a way to review their effects. The result is a usable session or asynchronous review packet and concrete improvement proposals, not a generic list of lessons learned.

Use [project-update-brief](../project-update-brief/SKILL.md) for progress reporting, [incident-evidence-timeline](../incident-evidence-timeline/SKILL.md) for reconstructing an incident, and [workflow-effectiveness-review](../workflow-effectiveness-review/SKILL.md) for comparing measured baseline and assisted runs. This workflow prepares team reflection and follow-through. It does not conduct a personnel assessment, establish misconduct, run a trial or claim a causal productivity improvement.

Use [design-quantitative-experiment](../design-quantitative-experiment/SKILL.md) when the requested result is a prospective quantitative protocol; local improvement cards here do not establish a causal effect.

## Establish the period, purpose and participation boundary

Read the user's requested team/project boundary, work period, desired decision and existing retrospective format. Identify:

- The completed or ending work interval, source cutoff and timezone where timestamps matter
- Available work artifacts, relevant milestones, supplied participant feedback and earlier improvement actions
- Intended participants and facilitator when known, with the actual audience for the final output
- Live or asynchronous format, known time/effort budget and any access or participation constraints
- Who can approve process changes, which changes the team can make, and existing review rules
- Requested output destination and whether to prepare a session, reconcile supplied outcomes or update a prior packet

A short, well-scoped retrospective can use a few supplied notes. Do not require calendar access, a survey, recordings or a full project export. Missing evidence should limit the affected finding rather than stop all preparation. Without participant input, write neutral prompts and tentative topics; do not invent what teammates felt or agreed.

Set an honest information boundary. Preserve existing confidentiality and retention instructions; do not promise anonymity because names were removed. Small-team details, quotations and roles can identify people. Use minimized paraphrases in shared material where exact quotations are unnecessary, and keep restricted source notes separate. Do not collect health details, private relationships or personal explanations for availability.

Sensitive allegations, disciplinary issues or immediate safety concerns should not become a group exercise or a public improvement card. Keep unnecessary identifying details out, flag the need for the user's appropriate existing process, and ask before any consequential disclosure. Do not investigate motives, infer protected traits or rank individuals from work logs. Reading feedback does not authorize contacting contributors or sharing it with a broader audience.

## Reconcile what happened and what was already tried

Build a small evidence spine for the period: intended outcomes, material events, useful results, friction, relevant constraints and unresolved contradictions. Use exact artifact or passage references, date and version when they affect the meaning. Distinguish work completed during the period from a later report about it.

Keep these layers separate:

- **Observation:** a traceable event or artifact, such as three review requests awaiting an owner at the cutoff
- **Reported experience:** someone's account, attributed or safely paraphrased within the permitted audience
- **Interpretation:** a proposed explanation, such as unclear ownership contributing to delay
- **Change idea:** an action the team might try, not an accepted commitment

Do not turn repeated retellings into independent incidents, a loud discussion into prevalence, or a missing source into proof that nothing happened. Preserve successes and counterexamples alongside difficulties. If counts are useful, state what is counted, which source set and period it covers, and what remains unknown. Message volume, online status and meeting attendance are not measures of individual effort or competence.

Revisit prior improvement actions before proposing replacements. Record whether an action was agreed, attempted, completed and actually reviewed; these are distinct states. A checklist being introduced does not prove it reduced rework. Retain abandoned or blocked trials and their reasons instead of repeatedly proposing the same change as new.

Choose topics because they affect the team's work and admit a useful discussion or next step. Do not force every observation into a negative theme, declare a root cause from chronology, or reduce disagreement to a net sentiment score. A useful question may remain unanswered after the retrospective.

## Author prompts and materials that produce a result

Provide the actual short pre-read and facilitation guide or asynchronous prompts. The [Scrum Guide's retrospective section](https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html#sprint-retrospective) connects inspection of past work with identifying useful changes. That supports the reflection-to-action purpose here; do not impose Scrum roles, Sprint rules or its timeboxes on a team using another process.

Make each stage clear about its input and output:

1. **Check the account:** Which facts need correction? What important work or success is missing? Output: an agreed account where supported, with unresolved differences retained
2. **Understand a specific friction:** What happened in this example? What conditions made it easier or harder? What evidence would distinguish competing explanations? Output: a bounded problem and explicit hypotheses
3. **Consider changes:** What can this team change within its authority? What useful practice should it preserve? Output: concrete candidate actions and constraints
4. **Choose the next step:** Which candidate is worth trying given current capacity, consequences and uncertainty? Who can approve it? Output: proposed or evidenced accepted trial cards
5. **Define review:** What will be observed, where will the result be recorded, and what decision will follow? Output: a usable review condition and open dependencies

Adapt these to the user's format. A live guide should give the facilitator exact opening wording, core questions, transitions and a place to capture decisions. An asynchronous packet should explain how to respond, what version is under review and when input closes if a deadline is supplied. Do not deliver only an agenda when actual prompts or worksheets were requested.

Offer a way to contribute without forcing a public personal disclosure, such as optional written input within an already authorized channel. Do not promise that a shared document, form or AI note-taker is anonymous or confidential beyond its actual settings. Recording, sending prompts and opening collection tools are separate actions; preparing the packet does not perform them.

Timebox only when a real session budget exists. Reserve useful space for selecting and defining actions rather than spending all of it collecting complaints. The sum of agenda estimates is a planning check, not proof that a discussion will finish on time. If the scope is too large, propose a smaller focus and retain the other topics for a stated later decision, without booking a follow-up.

## Turn a selected idea into a bounded improvement trial

Select only as many changes as the team's stated capacity can support. If no team choice has occurred, offer a reasoned shortlist and label it proposed. Popularity can help reveal interest when the team's process uses it, but is not evidence of cause, permission or likely benefit. Do not invent votes or make a consequential tradeoff on the team's behalf.

For each selected or proposed change, write a practical card containing:

- **Problem and evidence:** the concrete behavior to improve and a source example
- **Hypothesis:** why this change might help, with competing explanations still visible
- **Action and scope:** the exact difference from current practice, affected workflow, exclusions and prerequisites
- **Ownership and authority:** an accepted owner or proposed role, decision needed and any access or policy dependency
- **Observation:** the outcome and guardrail to record, with units, start/end boundaries and a modest collection method
- **Trial and review boundary:** the dates or work opportunities that define the attempt, and the specific point at which results will be reviewed
- **Decision and recovery:** what would support keeping, adapting or stopping the change, and how to return safely to the prior practice

Prefer a small observable process change to “communicate better.” Do not treat a full rollout, new paid tool, surveillance system or weakened quality gate as an ordinary retrospective action. Such proposals remain gated by their actual cost, privacy, safety and implementation authority.

Use existing operational evidence where possible. If comparing review delay, define whether the clock starts when a complete request is submitted and ends at acknowledgment or at a substantive decision; those are different outcomes. Include unfinished requests at the cutoff rather than measuring only fast completed ones. Keep effort, calendar duration and working-time duration separate.

If no comparable baseline exists, say so. The first bounded observation period may establish a description rather than an improvement estimate. Define a review decision even when numerical thresholds are not justified: examine whether the agreed examples were handled and whether the guardrail was breached. Do not invent a target percentage, a historical baseline or a statistically meaningful sample size.

State practical stop conditions, such as exceeding an approved workload limit or blocking a required review. A stop condition must not authorize unapproved changes to a live system. If several changes run together, record that attribution is limited; a later difference cannot automatically be credited to one clause.

## Keep decisions, execution and outcomes separate

Prepare a lightweight review record with the card/version, decision status, actual start/end or opportunity count, observations, missing data, guardrail results and the eventual keep/adapt/stop decision. Leave future outcomes blank. Do not mark a trial running because its planned start date arrived.

When later results are supplied, reconcile them against the same action, population, clock and quality conditions. Preserve changes made during the trial, incomplete work and contradictory experiences. A completed action can have an unknown or disappointing effect. A before/after difference can inform a local decision without proving that the intervention caused it.

Record an accepted change only from the team's actual authority and decision evidence. A facilitator's summary, chat reaction or absence of objections does not automatically adopt a process. If important conditions changed, update the proposal and identify which prior decisions need reconsideration rather than carrying approval into a materially different trial.

## Acceptance, delivery and recovery

Deliver the requested facilitation or asynchronous packet, concrete trial cards and a blank or evidence-filled review record as appropriate. Keep it short enough to use. A small team may need one page of prompts and one card; do not add an elaborate improvement program to a bounded request.

Before delivery, verify:

- Each factual claim and quotation has an inspected source or is labeled reported/unknown
- Prompts allow contrary evidence and do not assume a person or hypothesis is at fault
- Prior actions distinguish implementation from observed effect
- Each trial has a real action, scope, owner status, reviewable observation and decision/recovery boundary
- Measurements retain their units, inclusion rule, missing outcomes and quality guardrails
- Shared material excludes private or consequential details outside the intended audience
- No prepared card, agenda or review date has become a claimed session, accepted assignment, live experiment or measured result

Read back saved materials and preserve the original notes, prior cards and actual decisions. If a source or destination is unavailable, return the usable portions and the exact remaining gap. Reconcile concurrent edits before replacing a packet. Inspect an uncertain save or authorized delivery before retrying.

Stop at the requested preparation or evidence-based review. Contacting participants, conducting a session, changing a workflow, assigning tasks, creating reminders or gathering ongoing measurements requires its own scope and authority. A request to prepare a retrospective is satisfied by usable materials and explicit decisions, not by fabricated agreement or an unrequested trial.

## Fictional, unexecuted illustration

A fictional publishing team supplies four review-request records. Two lacked an exact draft version. The records do not establish whether that omission caused their delays. The prior action “add a review checklist” was completed, but nobody recorded whether requesters used it.

The packet asks what information reviewers actually needed and whether complete requests were also delayed. A proposed trial adds a draft-version field to the existing request template for the next six eligible requests. Its observation record includes every eligible request, missing-field status, substantive-review timing and unresolved requests at the cutoff. The owner is proposed, and adoption is pending. A guardrail preserves the existing factual review; the trial cannot shorten that review to make response times look better.

Six opportunities are an invented planning boundary, not a statistically justified sample. No session occurred, template changed, task assigned, data collected or benefit measured in this illustration.
