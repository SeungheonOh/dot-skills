# Create a glossary that resolves real confusion

Deliver the actual reader-facing reference, not just an extracted word list. Select the terms that impede understanding, define their meanings in the relevant setting and help the reader distinguish nearby concepts. The glossary should work when opened at one entry rather than only when read from beginning to end.

Use [package-terminology-exchange](../package-terminology-exchange/SKILL.md) for transferring an existing multilingual termbase, [create-subject-index](../create-subject-index/SKILL.md) for locating discussions in a document, and [design-classification-scheme](../design-classification-scheme/SKILL.md) for rules that assign material to categories. This workflow authors explanations. It does not approve official terminology, translate a publication or replace a complete lesson or operating procedure.

## Define the reader and source boundary

Identify the intended audience, subject or product scope, source material and versions, language, expected length and requested destination. Existing questions, unfamiliar expressions in a supplied document and the user's examples of confusion are better starting points than a large indiscriminate list. Ask only when a missing audience or meaning would materially change an entry.

Use a supplied source-only boundary when requested. Otherwise retrieve authoritative sources only as needed to establish meanings, especially for current product terminology. Keep private document wording and internal project names out of public queries unless authorized. A source title or search snippet may locate a definition but cannot support details not inspected.

Preserve originals and existing accepted definitions. Record the exact source edition or revision that governs the glossary. An older usage can be appropriate for a historical text; do not silently replace it with current usage. If source sections are unavailable, mark affected entries unresolved and describe the coverage limit instead of filling them with plausible general knowledge.

## Select terms by their reader value

Read the in-scope material in context, including labels, figures, footnotes and examples when they affect meaning. Select jargon, abbreviations, ordinary words used in a specialized sense, and terms readers are likely to confuse. Frequency alone is insufficient: a rare term can determine the whole argument, while a frequent everyday word may need no entry.

Keep a lightweight term-to-source map. It should identify the expression, source occurrences, intended concept or sense, why explanation is useful and any ambiguity. A short glossary can keep this evidence in entry source notes rather than a separate ledger.

Do not assume one spelling equals one meaning. Qualify entries when the same expression names distinct concepts in different settings. Conversely, a full form and acronym may share an entry when the source establishes equivalence. Similar-looking acronyms, products and versions remain distinct until their relationship is supported. Never invent an expansion to complete an abbreviation list.

Separate a preferred display label from claims about official usage. Choose a readable headword for navigation without declaring other accepted expressions incorrect. Keep deprecated, prohibited or official labels only when an applicable authority actually states them. A source's frequent wording is evidence of usage, not evidence that alternatives are forbidden.

## Write definitions that preserve the distinction

Build each substantive entry around the reader's actual difficulty. A useful entry can contain a headword and scope qualifier, a concise definition, a consequential boundary or contrast, a short original example when it clarifies meaning, relevant aliases and source locators. Do not require every field for every word.

Begin with a familiar category or function, then the property that distinguishes the concept in this setting. Avoid merely expanding an acronym, substituting another unexplained technical word, or defining a term only by repeating it. A definition must not depend on the reader already knowing the concept it explains.

Preserve conditions, units and level of abstraction. Distinguish a method from its result, a measurement from the property measured, a requirement from an example, and a permission from an obligation. An analogy can aid comprehension but must not become a factual mechanism. Label the point where an analogy stops fitting when that limitation matters.

Use examples and contrasts deliberately:

- An original hypothetical example illustrates the definition; it is not a claim that the source documented that event
- A contrasting example should differ in the feature the definition depends on, rather than introduce several unrelated changes
- “Often associated with” is weaker than “means the same as”; do not turn a correlation or typical use into an equivalence
- A broader or narrower concept is not automatically a synonym; retain separate entries when the distinction helps the reader

When adjacent sources use different definitions, compare their version, discipline, audience and operational setting. If the difference is legitimate, give scoped senses or clearly attributed alternatives. If the intended governing meaning remains uncertain, hold that choice instead of blending incompatible definitions into a smooth but unsupported sentence.

For formal legal, medical, financial, safety or standards-defined terms, preserve the applicable source language and scope where exactness matters, with a clearly separated plain-language explanation. Do not turn the glossary into professional advice, infer obligations for a particular person or invent a universal definition from one jurisdiction or standard. Escalate a consequential interpretation to the appropriate qualified reviewer when needed.

## Make standalone lookup work

Choose an order and navigation suited to use: alphabetical lookup, a small concept grouping, or both. Preserve the requested language and established collation convention. Keep entries discoverable through common aliases or abbreviations without duplicating the complete definition under each label.

Use redirects only for true aliases in the stated scope. Point them to a real substantive entry. For related but distinct concepts, provide a short explanation of the relationship instead of an unexplained chain of “see also” links. Avoid circular definitions and alias loops. A reader reaching an abbreviation should reach its definition directly, with the full form visible.

Give maintained entries stable IDs when the artifact will be revised or linked from other documents. A renamed headword can keep its identity; a split into different senses needs explicit mapping from the previous entry. Do not silently repurpose an old link for a different meaning. In a static file, verify anchors and cross-references against the saved output rather than assuming they follow the editor's heading rules.

Keep the main explanation concise enough to scan, with optional detail separated from the definition. If a term requires a long prerequisite lesson or operating instructions, give a faithful compact explanation and link to that material; do not bury a full tutorial in the glossary. An entry must still provide some useful meaning rather than serve only as a link to another long document.

## Preserve attribution, rights and approval

Write original explanations grounded in inspected sources. Use exact quotations only where wording matters and keep them appropriately short. Consider aggregate copying across the glossary; hundreds of short dictionary definitions can still reproduce substantial protected material. Prefer a new explanatory structure and source locators over copying an existing glossary wholesale. Preserve known attribution and rights notices without inventing a reuse license.

Keep internal acronyms, project definitions, personal examples and source links within the requested audience. An apparently harmless glossary can reveal confidential plans or relationships. Do not upload restricted source text to a new terminology service, make a public page or change shared-document access as part of preparing it. A public derivative should contain only the approved content and links its readers may use.

Distinguish proposed explanatory wording from approved organization language. A request to draft a glossary authorizes drafting, not imposing terminology on other documents or accepting the draft on behalf of its owner. Preserve required definitions verbatim where instructed and flag proposed changes separately. Publication and live termbase updates require their own authority.

## Check, save and maintain

Review each definition against its supporting passages, including qualifiers that would change its meaning. Check that an example satisfies the definition and a deliberate near-miss actually fails for the stated reason. Compare adjacent entries for accidental overlap, inconsistent scope and contradictory explanations. Keep unseen source material out of the support chain.

Use concrete lookup questions rather than a count of entries as the acceptance check. Can the intended reader find the term from its acronym or common label, distinguish a confusing pair and understand the definition without following a circular reference? A self-review can establish these editorial properties; it does not prove actual reader comprehension or independent usability results.

Save the requested readable reference and any necessary source/maintenance map to the authorized destination. Read back the saved artifact. Check all required entries and cross-reference targets, ambiguous labels, non-ASCII terms, source links and any affected layout. If a native format or publication target cannot be inspected, deliver a clearly labeled candidate and the exact remaining check rather than claiming it is ready there.

For an update, compare source and destination revisions before writing. Preserve intervening edits and approved wording. If a source meaning changes, revise affected entries, examples, aliases and inbound references together; a wording-only rename should not alter concept identity. Keep a brief record of consequential definition changes, not merely a new date on the cover. A historical glossary remains tied to its edition unless the user requests modernization.

On uncertain save, inspect the destination before retrying. When an entry proves ambiguous, retain the source evidence, split or qualify the senses, and recheck its redirects rather than silently choosing one. If decisive evidence is unavailable, leave that entry explicitly incomplete while finishing independent entries. Stop with the usable glossary and named unresolved terms; do not claim completeness beyond the inspected scope or start recurring updates.

## Fictional, unexecuted illustration

A fictional volunteer handbook uses “reserve” in two ways: a room held pending confirmation and a backup volunteer available if needed. It also uses “confirmed” for a room booking that the venue has accepted. The supplied handbook explicitly distinguishes a provisional room hold from an accepted booking.

The proposed glossary has “Reserve, room” and “Reserve, volunteer” as separate entries. The room entry explains that a hold preserves an option under the handbook's process and does not establish confirmation. “Confirmed room” points to the evidenced acceptance state. A hypothetical room request with no venue response is a contrast example, not a confirmed reservation. The glossary does not invent an expiry period or treat the two uses of reserve as synonyms.

This is an original fictional editorial illustration. No handbook, venue record, native document, publication or reader trial was used or executed.
